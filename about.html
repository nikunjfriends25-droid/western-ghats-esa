What this is

On 31 July 2024 the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change published draft notification S.O. 3060(E) (PDF, 4.9 MB, 279 pages), listing 4,402 villages across Goa, Gujarat, Karnataka, Kerala, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu proposed as an Ecologically Sensitive Area in the Western Ghats. The notification names those villages in a table. It does not publish their boundaries.

This atlas reconstructs them. Every village named in Annexure C was matched to its polygon in the all-India census village boundary dataset, giving 4,331 of 4,402 rows (98.4%) a mapped extent carrying its 2011 Census code and LGD code.

Reading the gazette

The village list and the boundary coordinates are read directly from the text layer of the notification published by MoEFCC, so every name and every coordinate is taken exactly as printed rather than recognised from an image.

Download the notification — PDF, 4.9 MB ↓

279 pages, hosted by MoEFCC at moef.gov.in/storage/tender/1723024218.pdf. Everything in this atlas is derived from that file.

Two checks confirm nothing was dropped in the reading. All six state serial runs reconcile exactly and contiguously — Goa 108, Gujarat 64, Karnataka 1,449, Kerala 131, Maharashtra 2,515, Tamil Nadu 135, giving 4,402 rows, which is the count the notification itself states. Annexure B yields 401 boundary points, and all 402 coordinate pairs printed in it are accounted for: the single one excluded is a typo in the gazette, Karnataka point 45, which reads 71.2007, 31.6736 — a location in Punjab, some 2,000 km from the Western Ghats.

Matching names to polygons

Village names were matched to census records hierarchically — district and taluka first, then district, then state-wide — under a strict 1:1 constraint, so no polygon is ever assigned to two gazette rows.

Three things made the difference over a naive fuzzy match:

  • Each census village offers three romanised spellings (name, censusname, lgd_villagename) as match aliases.
  • Both sides are additionally compared under a transliteration folding (oo→u, ee→i, doubled consonants collapsed, trailing vowels dropped). Without it, Marayur against Marayoor scores only 80 and is rejected; with it, the pair is effectively identical.
  • Thresholds scale with how much the search was narrowed. District-plus-taluka already cuts the pool to a handful of villages — Devikulam taluka has twelve — so it runs a looser threshold (72) than a district-wide (85) or state-wide (93) search.

A name can match perfectly in the wrong place. The gazette's Khadi in Surat scored a perfect 100 against Khada in Junagadh — 295 km away in Saurashtra, nowhere near the Western Ghats. Raigad's Wandoli matched a village in Gadchiroli, 631 km across Maharashtra.

So a geographic gate is applied: villages matched at district-and-taluka scope define each state's ESA core, and any wider-scope match landing more than 50 km outside that core is rejected and retried against its remaining candidates. Thirty outliers were caught; twenty-three found a correct nearer village instead. No match now sits beyond 50 km of its state core.

Three independent verifications

A village-name match can be confidently wrong, so the result is checked three ways that do not share a failure mode.

1 · Against the gazette's own coordinates

Annexure B lists 401 "prominent points on the outer boundary" of the ESA. These are boundary points, so the correct test is how far each lies from the edge of the reconstructed polygon — not whether it falls inside. A point sitting exactly on a boundary is a coin-flip for a point-in-polygon test, and roughly half of them do fall inside, which is the expected signature.

2 · Against Kerala's own published ESA

Kerala released its ESA as KML — an independent government source for the same ground. The direction of this test matters, so stated precisely:

  • 98.1% of the official Kerala area falls inside these village polygons. Only 170 km² of it lands outside — and that residue is spread across 833 fragments of which 820 are smaller than 1 km², i.e. sliver mismatches along shared edges rather than any missing region. 67 of its 98 polygons are more than 99% covered, 86 are more than 95% covered.
  • 66.7% of these village polygons fall inside the official area. The other third is the part of each village that was not notified.

Both numbers are correct and they measure opposite things. The first says the official ESA is almost entirely contained by this reconstruction — which is the test that matters. The second is low by design, because Kerala notified portions of villages while this atlas holds whole villages; a whole village is necessarily bigger than the slice of it that was listed.

On the map this looks counter-intuitive at first. Switch on the Kerala official layer and the blue outline sits inside the purple village polygons, with purple spilling beyond it — that spill is the 33%, not a mismatch. If the blue appeared to extend past the purple, that would be the failure. It does not.

Where it is weakest

Bifurcated villages are drawn whole. The gazette marks some villages with an asterisk, meaning only part of the village falls inside the ESA. No partial extent is published anywhere, so those polygons show the entire village and therefore over-state the ESA. Only the State Governments' physical-verification maps can resolve them. The atlas flags every such village when you open it.

Kerala is portion-wise by design. This is why Kerala's ratio is 1.30 rather than 1.00 — it is not an error in the matching. For Kerala, treat the official state KML layer as authoritative and these village polygons as context. You can overlay the official layer directly in the atlas.

Karnataka is the least complete state, at 0.86 of its notified area, with 19 rows unmapped. Its census transliterations diverge from the gazette's more than any other state's.

The 71 unmapped rows are mostly structural. They are composite cells naming two features at once (Amravathi R.F./Anamalai R.F.), gazette rows repeated for different survey blocks (Valparai (TP) appears four times, and the 1:1 constraint gives the polygon to the first occurrence), placeholder text (Area Under Forest), and small Raigad hamlets below census village granularity. The full list, with a reason for each, is in the atlas under "Not mapped".

This is not an official record. It is an independent reconstruction for reference and research, with no legal standing. It must not be used to determine whether a specific parcel of land falls inside the ESA. For that, consult the notification itself and the relevant State Government.

Why there are no Zone 1/2/3

A common expectation is that the Western Ghats ESA is graded into Zone 1, Zone 2 and Zone 3. It is not — and the distinction matters if you are comparing this atlas against older maps or reports.

The graded scheme comes from the Western Ghats Ecology Expert Panel (the Gadgil report, 2011), which proposed dividing the range into ESZ 1, ESZ 2 and ESZ 3 with progressively lighter restrictions. That scheme was never notified.

What followed was the High Level Working Group (the Kasturirangan report, submitted 15 April 2013), which the 2024 draft notification is built on. It took a different approach: rather than grading the whole range, it identified roughly 37% of the Western Ghats — 59,940 km² of natural landscape — as a single continuous ecologically sensitive band, and left everything outside it unclassified.

So S.O. 3060(E) defines one undivided ESA. Searching the full 279-page notification for zone terminology returns nothing: the only occurrences of "ESZ" are an email address (esz-mef@nic.in) and the file number F.No.1/04/2012-ESZ-RE. The only "categories" it names are categories of projects and activities that are prohibited or regulated, and the CPCB's Red/Orange/White industry categories — neither of which is spatial.

Consequently the categories in this atlas are not statutory zones. They record how each village was mapped and how much to trust its extent — nothing about differing levels of legal protection. Every village shown is subject to the same single set of ESA restrictions.

What the categories mean

Every village in the atlas carries one of five categories. They are not levels of legal protection — as above, the ESA is single and undivided, and every village shown is subject to the same restrictions. What the category tells you is how that polygon was arrived at, and how much to trust its extent. Two of the five are warnings.

Whole village — 4,131 villages, 40,850 km²

The ordinary case, and about 73% of the mapped area. The gazette names the village without qualification, so the entire revenue village falls inside the ESA and the polygon is simply that village's census boundary. Nothing is being inferred beyond the name match itself, so the extent is as good as the census boundary is.

Bifurcated — 26 villages, 111 km² (treat as an over-estimate)

The gazette marks some village names with an asterisk. That marker means only part of the village lies inside the ESA — the boundary cuts through it.

The notification does not publish where that cut falls. No annexure, no coordinates, no sub-village list. So the honest options were to drop these villages entirely or to draw them whole and label them clearly. This atlas draws them whole, which means every one of these polygons is larger than the ESA actually is. The map flags it when you open one.

The scale is small — these 26 cover 111 km², well under 1% of the mapped area — so the effect on any regional total is minor. For a specific village it is not minor at all. Only the State Governments' physical-verification maps can resolve the true extents.

All 26 are in Goa (9) and Maharashtra (17). The gazette marks 54 villages this way in total; the remaining 27 are mostly small Raigad hamlets that have no census village polygon at all, and appear under Not mapped instead.

Kerala village — 122 villages, 12,360 km² (use the official layer)

Every Kerala row carries this category, because Kerala is a special case. The State defines its ESA portion-wise: not "this village is in", but "this part of this village is in". The gazette lists the village names; Kerala publishes the actual portions separately as KML.

So these 122 polygons are whole villages standing in for what is really a set of partial ones. That is the entire reason Kerala's area ratio is 1.30 rather than 1.00 — it is not a matching error, it is the difference between two definitions.

Kerala's official layer is included in the atlas. Switch on Kerala official ESA under Reference layers to see the real boundary sitting inside these village polygons, and treat that layer as authoritative for Kerala.

Notified forest — 51 areas, 2,263 km²

Not revenue villages at all. These are Reserve Forests and forest blocks that the gazette lists in the same table as villages — entries like Thadagam R.F., Anaikatti North R.F. and Jaganarai Slopes R.F. They are identified by the R.F. suffix the notification uses.

All 51 are in Tamil Nadu, which lists forest blocks far more than the other five states do. They are mappable here because the census boundary dataset carries Reserve Forests as features in their own right. They range from 1.5 km² to 365 km², so a few of them are large.

Kerala, bifurcated — 1 village, 670 km²

A single village, Chittar, that is both a Kerala row and asterisk-marked in the gazette — so both caveats apply at once. It is worth its own category because it is large: at 670 km² it is by far the biggest polygon carrying a partial-extent warning, and it alone accounts for most of the 781 km² of asterisk-marked area in the dataset.

In the data files these appear in the esa_category field as whole_village, bifurcated_village, kerala_village, notified_forest and kerala_bifurcated_village. The separate boolean bifurcated field is the quickest way to filter out every partial-extent polygon in one step.

The analysis layer

The reconstruction above is a transcription of a legal document. Everything in this section is derived — the ESA polygons joined to census, land-cover, protection and hydrology datasets. It is a different kind of claim, and it carries the errors of every layer it touches, so it is kept separate and its limits are listed in full.

Census figures join on the 2011 village code, matching 4,297 of 4,331 villages. Areas are measured in EPSG:7755. Where a source layer contains overlapping polygons, its geometry is unioned before measurement — summing per-feature intersections double-counted, in one case reporting 154% of a village as recorded forest.

The composite scores

Two scores, each the mean of percentile-ranked components, published with their inputs so the weighting can be disputed and recomputed:

  • Conservation value — natural forest %, corridor/tiger reserve %, recorded forest %
  • Human pressure — population density, built-up %, highway density, land-dependent workers %

An earlier version put population density on both axes (inverted on one) and set recorded forest against its own arithmetic complement, which forced 86% of villages onto a single diagonal — the quadrants were an artefact of the formula rather than a finding. Land cover turned out to be compositional too: natural forest and agriculture correlate −0.80 because the classes sum to 100%, so using both was one variable counted twice with opposite signs. Pressure now measures people and infrastructure only. The two scores correlate −0.48: related, as you would expect, but not mirror images.

Read these before quoting any derived number.

Whole-village inflation runs through everything. Bifurcated and all Kerala villages are whole-village polygons standing in for partial extents. Population is therefore an upper bound — and 39% of the population total comes from Kerala, where extent is over-stated by roughly 30%. Conversely every percentage-of-area figure is an under-estimate, because the denominator is inflated while the numerators are real.

Land outside Recorded Forest Area is not private land. It also holds revenue and common land, water bodies, PSU and defence land, and parks notified on non-forest land — plus gaps in the forest layer itself. 346 villages record no forest area despite being more than half forest; those are layer gaps, not tenure findings.

Land cover is unreliable for small villages. NRSC LULC is mapped at 1:250,000 with 112–168 m registration error. For a village under about 25 km² that band is comparable to its whole area. Only 416 of 4,331 villages clear that bar, though they hold 52% of the area; the rest are flagged as indicative.

Protected areas are OpenStreetMap-derived, not the official WII boundaries, so the 28% protected figure carries unquantified error.

"Mining" means derelict land, not active leases. The Wasteland Atlas class is abandoned industrial and mining land as of 2015-16. The 15 km² it finds across the whole ESA is far too little to represent active extraction, and it is excluded from scoring.
